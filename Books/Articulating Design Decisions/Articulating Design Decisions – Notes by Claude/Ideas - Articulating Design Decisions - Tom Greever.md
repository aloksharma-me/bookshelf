# Ideas — Articulating Design Decisions (Tom Greever)

Source: [[Articulating Design Decisions - Tom Greever]]

Ideas, claims, and experiments from the highlights worth exploring further — a research to-do list seeded by the book.

- **"The most articulate person usually wins."** Greever's central claim that communication, not design talent, decides whose ideas ship. *Next step:* test against your own past projects — list decisions that went your way vs. not, and note whether articulation or design quality drove the outcome.

- **The Big Three as a design checklist.** For every decision, write down: What problem does it solve? How does it affect the user? Why is it better than the alternative? *Next step:* turn this into a reusable template (e.g., a Notion/Obsidian note or design-doc section) and apply it to a live project.

- **Write down every design decision and its "why."** Greever argues the act of writing reveals your own hidden motivations and connects problem to solution. *Next step:* keep a decision log for one project sprint and review whether it improved your meeting articulation.

- **Anticipate reactions as a near-formula:** personality + role/values + observed reactions ≈ predictable behavior. *Next step:* build a simple stakeholder-map worksheet (per person: what they care about, likely objections, your prepared response).

- **Design the meeting for memory** using primacy/recency, repetition (state the goal 3×), surprise, and mixed media. *Next step:* explore the cognitive-science literature on serial-position effect and spaced repetition to validate/refine the tactics; experiment with one "surprise" device in a real meeting.

- **Five response tactics:** show a comparison, propose an alternative, give them a choice, ask others to weigh in, postpone the decision. *Next step:* make flashcards or a one-page cheat sheet to rehearse picking the right tactic in the moment.

- **"Give them a choice" exploits loss aversion** — fear of loss outweighs promise of gain. *Next step:* read the Kahneman & Tversky work on prospect theory to understand why, and find ethical vs. manipulative lines for using it on stakeholders.

- **The IDEA → IDEAL framework** for locking in agreement, ending with a direct "Do you agree?" *Next step:* practice the explicit agreement-close; track whether decisions "re-litigate" less in subsequent meetings.

- **The Bank Account of Trust.** Every interaction is a deposit or withdrawal; the long-term goal is a default of trust. *Next step:* identify which "battles" are worth a withdrawal on your current projects.

- **Three red flags that you're wrong:** the problem still exists, users don't get it, you don't have support. *Next step:* adopt these as a personal post-launch retro checklist.

- **"Do something, even if it's wrong"** — making an obviously bad decision can be the fastest way to force stakeholder engagement. *Next step:* a risky idea worth examining — consider where forcing a decision via a deliberate strawman helps vs. damages trust.

- **The Design Project Checklist** (Vision/Goals, Users, Workflow/Communication, Access, Design/Technical Requirements). *Next step:* convert into a kickoff questionnaire you send before starting any new project.

- **"Hairy arms" and "the duck" as influence tactics.** *Next step:* research the historical accuracy of both anecdotes (Disney animation; Interplay's Battle Chess via Jeff Atwood) and reflect on the ethics of deliberately planting removable distractions.

- **Executives' "Ten Tips for Working with Designers."** A rare artifact written *for stakeholders.* *Next step:* adapt it into a short one-pager to share with your own managers/clients to set collaboration norms.
