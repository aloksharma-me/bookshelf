Chapters at a Glance

Click any chapter to expand its key takeaways. Expand all

Foundation · Why & Who
  • All design is subjective — there’s little shared understanding of what “good” means, which is why it’s so hard to talk about.
  • A design only succeeds when it does three things: solves a problem, is easy for users, and is supported by everyone.
  • Being articulate imparts intelligence, demonstrates intentionality, expresses confidence, and shows respect.
  • The discipline: for every decision, write down what problem it solves, how it affects the user, and why it beats the alternative.
  • Everyone is unique — tailor how you engage by role and values.
  • Executives: bring them up to speed fast, present, solicit feedback. Developers: analytical; may not even “see” UI details.
  • Also map product owners, project managers, and marketing/content/creative — each cares about different things.
  • Knowing what each person values lets you predict — and prepare for — their reactions.
Before · Design the Meeting
  • Set the context: state the goal, recap last time, show a timeline, specify the feedback you need — then state the goal again.
  • Optimize for memory: primacy & recency, repeat the goal 3×, add surprise, use mixed media.
  • Anticipate reactions: write down likely objections, prepare alternatives, and have data ready (but only use it if needed).
  • Build a support network and do a dress rehearsal — practicing out loud is “the usability test of being articulate.”
In the room · Listen → Respond → Agree
  • Implicit listening: let them talk, hear what isn’t said, and master the pause before responding.
  • Uncover the real problem behind the request: “What problem are you trying to solve by suggesting this?”
  • Explicit listening: write everything down — notes prevent repeat conversations, build trust, and keep the meeting on track.
  • Repeat & rephrase: convert “likes/dislikes” into “works/doesn’t work,” and lead with “What I hear you saying…”
  • Your role is to lead a conversation, not absorb an onslaught of changes.
  • Give up control and check your ego — defensiveness is the enemy of a good response.
  • Lead with a YES (meetings are improv). Avoid “Yes, but…” — it’s just “no” in disguise.
  • Change your vocabulary; make a graceful transition with “Thank, Repeat, Prepare.”
  • Anchor every response in The Big Three: problem, user, alternative.
  • Strategies: appeal to a nobler motive, represent the user, demonstrate effectiveness (prototype it).
  • Five tactics: show a comparison · propose an alternative · give them a choice · ask others to weigh in · postpone the decision.
  • “Give them a choice” works because fear of loss outweighs the promise of gain.
  • Business: helps achieve a goal · facilitates a use case · communicates branding.
  • Design: uses a common pattern · draws the user’s attention · creates a flow for the user.
  • Research: validated by data · revealed in testing · supported by other research.
  • Limitations: not enough resources · limited by technology · complies with a standard.
  • The IDEAL Response: Identify the problem · Describe your solution · Empathize with the user · Appeal to the business · Lock in agreement.
  • Be direct: “Before we move on, are we all in agreement to move forward with this?”
  • Highlight the benefit in how you ask: “Do you agree we should improve conversion by removing these fields?”
  • A direct question forces a direct answer — preventing the decision from re-surfacing next meeting.
After · Follow Up & Adapt
  • The meeting after the meeting: stick around — the hallway is often the most productive place.
  • Follow up fast (within an hour): thank, recap decisions, and focus on actions & next steps.
  • Apply filters: not every idea deserves to be recorded — read intent, agreement, and influence.
  • Do something, even if it’s wrong: when stuck, make the call and communicate it with a deadline.
  • Beware bike-shedding (Parkinson’s Law of Triviality), and the lore of the duck and hairy arms.
  • Diagnose what’s really going on: misunderstanding · you’re wrong · an unmet need · they want to be heard.
  • Make lemonade — treat forced changes as a prompt for an even better solution.
  • The Bank Account of Trust: every interaction is a deposit or withdrawal. Learn which battles are worth fighting.
The other side · For Executives
  • Ten Tips: focus on what works · don’t hand over solutions · ask lots of questions · don’t claim to be the user · let designers explain.
  • Empower designers to decide · use helpful language · ask if there’s data · be prepared · give them what they need to succeed.
  • Design Project Checklist — kickoff questions across Vision/Goals, Users, Workflow/Communication, Access, and Design/Technical requirements.
  • Decide who makes the final call per role — “No committees.”

The Core Frameworks

The reusable engines that run underneath the chapters — worth memorising.

The Big Three

Answer these for every decision — to yourself first, then to stakeholders.

1What problem does it solve? Tie it to a measurable business goal.
2How does it affect the user? Common sense + research.
3Why is it better than the alternative? Keep your rejected options.

The IDEAL Response

How to deliver a response and close out agreement.

IIdentify the problem.
DDescribe your solution.
EEmpathize with the user.
AAppeal to the business.
LLock in agreement — ask “Do you agree?”

Five Response Tactics

Pick one in the moment when a change is suggested.

Show a comparisonSide by side Propose an alternativeAdd your expertise Give them a choiceLoss aversion Ask others to weigh inDirectly + neutrally Postpone the decisionBuy time to get it right

Thank · Repeat · Prepare

A one-sentence transition from their feedback to your response.

TThank them for their time and input.
RRepeat / summarise what they said.
PPrepare them for how you’ll respond.

The Bank Account of Trust

A model for the long game with every stakeholder.

+Deposits: agreeing, hitting metrics, delivering designs they love.
–Withdrawals: missed deadlines, refusing changes (even when you’re right).
★Goal: a balance so high their default is to trust your choices.

Tactics & Terms

The book’s memorable coinages and the jargon worth knowing.

🦆 Painting a duck

Deliberately add an obvious, removable element so stakeholders fixate on cutting it instead of the parts you care about. (Jeff Atwood / Interplay’s Battle Chess.)

💪 Hairy arms

Make an unwanted option subtly unappealing so the decision-maker rejects it for your preferred one. (1940s Disney animation lore.)

🔘 CEO button

An unexpected executive request to add a feature that destroys the balance of a project.

🏠 Home-page syndrome

The home screen becomes a dumping ground for every link, button, and banner — wrecking usability.

🚲 Bike-shedding

Spending disproportionate meeting time on a trivial, easily-understood issue. From Parkinson’s Law of Triviality.

↩️ Reflective data

Data collected after a change to compare before-and-after, vs. existing data already on hand.

👥 Design-by-committee

Design driven by merged, compromised suggestions rather than a coherent vision.

🤝 Relational capital

Goodwill and influence built within an organisation that can be leveraged to support a decision.

🎚️ Fidelity (low/high)

How detailed/polished an artifact is — from rough wireframes to ship-ready visuals.

🧠 Cognitive load

The mental effort to process information; good prep and notes reduce it for you and stakeholders.
