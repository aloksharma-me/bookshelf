# Chapter 1 – First Impressions Matter
- Reviewed thousands of portfolios over 16 years, shortlisted around 15, interviewed even fewer and hired only 5.
- Author (and other hiring managers) have maybe 10 seconds. They need to answer these questions in that time
	- What do you do?
	- Are you good at it?
	- Do I want to keep going?
- Some common mistakes he's seen in portfolios
	- Showing 6-10 case-studies with the same weight and importance. Best portfolios have 3-5 case-studies maximum. Strongest project first, then others.
	- Process dump in order – the challenge, research, personas, wireframes, iterations, final designs or prototype.
	- Corporate buzzwords like user-centric, holistic, stakeholder alignment
	- Mismatch between portfolio and job applying for.

### Tip 1 – Get to the point
- Nobody shows up eager to admire your process. They want a result worth caring about. The process only matters after the outcome earns a curiosity.
- Lead with the strongest signal, the clearest outcome – the piece of work that shows taste, judgement and restraint.
- Put your strongest work first instead of the most recent or the most thorough.

### Tip 2 – Stand out
- Most applications/portfolios blue together and look the same. Hiring managers need to see a difference.
- Remove guesswork for the hiring manager. Show how your process works for them.
- One way is to open the company's website/app and share a critique or suggest fixes.
- Hiring managers need to answer this question "If we hired this person, would they make things better?"

### Tip 3 – Show your quirks
- Professionalism has been flattened into a look – neutral typefaces, safe grids, muted colours. Everyone follows the same formula.
- People remember a sharp thought, a moment that feels human instead of rehearsed.
- "If photography trained your eye for framing and hierarchy, that belongs in a portfolio work. If games taught you about feedback loops and motivation, that's design thinking. If comics shaped how you explain complex ideas simply, that's a skill most teams desperately need."

### Tip 4 – Build for someone, not everyone
- If your work doesn't speak to the product, the constraints, or the taste of the company, the hiring managers won't be able to get a good answer for "Is this person good?", "Can I see them working here?"
- When a company works on consumer products, they care about clarity, scale, behaviour.
- When a company works on enterprise, they care about complexity, edge-cases and decision making under constraints.

### Tip 5 – Don't make a user manual
- Most case-studies fail because they confuse thoroughness with clarity. The primary work gets buried under artefacts, frameworks and processes.
- Case-studies need a hook – what changed because of this work? did a metric move? did behaviour shift? did something become simpler, faster or more reliable?
- Design the case-study to be scanned and skimmed. Compress the details with ruthless prioritisation.
	- What's the one sentence that explains why this project mattered?
	- If you had to explain this project in a tweet, what would you say?
	- If you removed half the words, would the meaning still hold?
	- If someone only saw a before & after image, would the improvement still hold?

### Tip 6 – Create a 'most proud of section'
- Cut the noise. Pick a project that actually counts. That tested you, pushed you, or changed something real.
- If it takes paragraphs of explanation to justify it, the framing is wrong or the work is not as strong as you think.

# Chapter 2 – Show, Don't Tell
- Perfect screens without context don't demonstrate skills.
- The market doesn't reward people who do a little of everything. It rewards people who've gone deep enough to develop judgement.
- Every portfolio claims to show process. Most of it is theatre – challenge, research, personas, wireframes, testing, iteration. That's not process, that's fiction.
- Real design is chaotic. It loops, stalls, breaks.
- What really matters are the moments when things fall apart. Like a user test that killed your favourite idea. The constraint you didn't see coming. These moments reflect whether you know how to recover when you're wrong.
- A portfolio with no failure isn't impressive.

### Tip 7 – Don't try to be the entire team
- Pick one skill position statement around it. Eg. "I design interactions that feel effortless" or "I make complex data understandable"
- Only show the projects that reinforce your niche.
- Be clear about your strengths but don't hid your range either.

### Tip 9 – Write better case studies
- Neither approach works – skipping process part entirely or documenting every sketch, wireframe and decision.
- Focus on turning points instead – what insight changed the direction of the design, what constraint forced you to think, what failed attempt taught you something useful?
- Take a case study and rewrite the introduction as an interesting story instead of a project summary.
- Treat case studies as an explanation of key decisions instead of a project archive. Hiring managers are looking for – how you think, how you prioritise, how you respond to constraints.

### Tip 10 – Show your unique method of working
- Hiring managers want to know how you approach your problems. Do you start with rough sketches, do you run a quick usability test, do you push back on a feature that didn't make sense.
- Show the turning points, the trade-offs, the feedback you acted on, the ideas you abandoned.
- Don't need to show everything, simply enough to make it clear the final solution didn't come out of nowhere.
- Add a 'How I got here' section to show key decisions, trade-offs, design progression, failed versions etc.

### Tip 11 – Show teamwork
- In your portfolio, be clear about what you did and also about what others contributed.
- Include discussions with engg that made design/experience better, co-creating features with PMs based on research, push backs from product/engg. that made the designs better.
- Include a "Biggest Challenge" section in case study that explains a team conflict and how you resolved it.
- Create a "Hand-off" section to show how you prepared designs for engineering and PMs.

### Tip 12 – Be transparent about failures
- [[Case studies on projects that didn't work out|Add a case-study that didn't work out]]. Walk through what went wrong, what you learned and how it changed your process.
- Pick a past design project where you struggled and write down three things you wish you had done differently.
- Take old design you created and redesign using what you're learnt since then. Compare the two versions and write a small paragraph reflecting on the differences.
