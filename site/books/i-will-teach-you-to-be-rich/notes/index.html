  • fixed costs should be 50–60 percent of your take-home pay.

Chapter 7: Investing isn't only for rich people

  • A Better Way to Invest: Automatic Investing
  • Automatic Investing is not some revolutionary technique that I just invented. It’s a simple way of investing in low-cost funds
  • It involves spending most of your time choosing how your money will be distributed in your portfolio, picking the investments (this actually takes the least amount of time), and then automating your regular investments
  • With Automatic Investing, you invest in low-cost funds—which replace worthless, expensive portfolio managers—and you save tens of thousands of pounds in trading fees, taxes, and overall investment expenses, outperforming most investors.
  • Asset allocation is your plan for investing, the way you organize the investments in your portfolio between stocks, bonds, and cash.
  • by diversifying your investments across different asset classes (like stocks and bonds, or, better yet, stock funds and bond funds), you could control the risk in your portfolio—and therefore control how much money, on average, you’d lose due to volatility.
  • Think about that remarkable fact: Your investment plan is more important than your actual investments.
  • by diversifying your investments, you’ll make more money as an individual investor.
  • This Pyramid of Investing Options represents your choices for different investments. At the bottom is the most basic level, where you can invest in stocks or bonds or just hold your money in cash. I’m oversimplifying because there are tons of different kinds of stocks and bonds, but you get the idea. Above them are tracker and actively managed funds. And finally, at the top of the pyramid, are lifestyle funds.
  • When you buy stock, you buy shares of a company. If the company does well, you expect your stock in it to do well.
  • Bonds are essentially IOUs from companies or the government.
  • Advantages of a managed fund: Hands-off approach means an expert money manager makes investment decisions for you. Managed funds hold many varied stocks, so if one company tanks, your fund doesn’t go down with it.
  • Tracker Funds:
  • tracker fund. These simple funds use computers to buy stocks and match the market (such as the S&P 500 or NASDAQ).
  • tracker funds are simply collections of stocks that computers manage in an effort to match the market.
  • Advantages of the tracker Fund: Extremely low cost, easy to maintain, and tax efficient.
  • Lifestyle Funds:
  • Lifecycle funds are simple funds that automatically diversify your investments for you based on age.
  • Lifecycle funds are actually “funds-of-funds,” or collections made up of other funds, which offer automatic diversification. For example, a lifecycle fund might include large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap, and international funds. (And those funds, in turn, will hold stocks from each of those areas.)
  • Default funds often have a lifestyle option, but they don’t all take the same approach. Some may not start moving money until five years before retirement.
  • aggressive fund (which will be a fund of mostly shares), balanced fund (this fund will contain shares and bonds), and cautious fund (a more conservative mix of mostly bonds).
  • The Rule of 72 is a fast trick you can do to figure out how long it will take to double your money. Here’s how it works: Divide the number 72 by the return rate you’re getting, and you’ll have the number of years you must invest in order to double your money.
  • the key to constructing a portfolio is not picking killer stocks! It’s figuring out a balanced asset allocation that will let you ride out storms and slowly grow, over time, to gargantuan proportions.
  • THE SWENSEN MODEL OF ASSET ALLOCATION 30 percent—Domestic equities: UK equity funds, including small-, mid-, and large-cap stocks 15 percent—Developed-world international equities: funds from developed foreign countries, including the United States, Germany, and France. 5 percent—Emerging-market equities: funds from developing foreign countries such as China, India, and Brazil. These are riskier than developed-world equities, so don’t go off buying these to fill 95 percent of your portfolio. 20 percent—Real estate funds: also known as REITs, short for real estate investment trust. REITs are funds that invest in residential and commercial property, both in the UK and overseas. 15 percent—Government bonds: also known as gilts, these provide predictable income and balance risk in your portfolio. As an asset class, bonds generally return less than stocks. 15 percent—Index-linked gilts: these bonds protect against inflation. Eventually you’ll want to own these, but they’d be the last ones I’d get after investing in all the better-returning options first.
  • Expense ratios are one of the few things you can control, and higher fees cost you dearly—and
  • dollar-cost averaging (that’s pound-cost averaging to you Brits) is a fancy phrase that refers to investing regular amounts over time, rather than investing all your money into a fund at once.
